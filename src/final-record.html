I was born in a house with a woodstove and a party line, and I have never once been bored.

Thirty-four years of fourth graders came through my classroom at Elm Street School. I learned their handwriting before their faces. Many of them are grandparents now, and some still send me a card at Christmas, which I keep in a drawer in the kitchen.

Harold and I were married in 1969 under the crab apple in his mother's yard. Two children, one boat that never quite floated, and a garden that outgrew us both. If you're wondering whether to share a memory below: please do. I would have read every word.

Obituary Published 6 May 2026 · print or share

Eleanor Ruth Vance, 80, of Camden, died peacefully at her home on Saturday, 2 May 2026, surrounded by her family.

Born 14 February 1946 on the Ducktrap Road in Lincolnville, she was the middle of five children of Walter and Ruth Sennett. She graduated from Camden High School in 1964 and from the University of Maine at Orono in 1968, the first in her family to earn a degree, and returned home to Knox County to teach.

Eleanor taught fourth grade at Elm Street School for thirty-four years, retiring in 2006. She married Harold Vance in 1969, and together they raised two children in the house on Chestnut Street. She gardened with strong opinions, sailed Penobscot Bay for forty years, and served eleven seasons with the Coast Guard Auxiliary. Generations of Camden children learned to read out loud in her classroom, and many of them never forgot it.

She was predeceased by her husband, Harold, in 2011. She is survived by her daughter, Kate Vance of Camden; her son, Thomas Vance of Portland; four grandchildren; and her sister, Margaret Sennett Pool of Belfast.

A gathering was held at the Camden Public Library on 16 May. A red oak has been planted in her memory at Ducktrap Grove in Lincolnville. In lieu of flowers, the family asks that you consider a gift to the Camden Public Library.

Eleanor was born in a farmhouse three miles up the Ducktrap Road, the middle of five, during a February cold enough that the midwife stayed two nights. She was the first in her family to finish high school and the first to leave the county for a degree. She came back anyway, which she always said was the more interesting decision.

She taught fourth grade at Elm Street School for thirty-four years. Eleanor believed children were capable of more than they often knew themselves. She encouraged nervous readers until their voices grew steadier, kept a chair by the classroom door for anyone who needed a quiet twenty minutes, and had a way of noticing what a child needed without making them feel noticed. For more than a decade, she quietly made sure winter boots found their way to families who needed them — something she never thought worth mentioning, even to Harold.

Outside the classroom, she was a gardener with strong opinions, an enthusiastic if unremarkable sailor, a Coast Guard Auxiliary volunteer for eleven seasons, and the person the street tended to call first when something went wrong. During the winter of 1998, she made room for three families in her home through nine days without power. Afterwards, she referred to the whole thing simply as “the long week.”

She died at home in Camden on the second of May, in the room with the good window, eighty years old and still annoyed about the crab apple tree. She left instructions for this page, a list of people to call, and one final note:

"Don’t make it sad. Make it accurate."
